There is no straightforward optimist in this cohort. The room divides into students who are ambivalent and students who are negative — and the ambivalence is not fence-sitting. It is a specific, repeated shape: the benefit first, the fear immediately after.

Anna is the clearest case. Her top-ranked opinion is that AI is practical for revising lectures. Her second is that relying on it damages our cognitive function. Sofia, Gilad, Marco and several anonymous participants do the same thing.

Benefits arrive first because they are what students use. Harms follow because they are what students believe.

Hopes and fears

Hopes, by weight

Time saved on low-value work, always described in the first person — summarising, revising, drafting, translating. Then AI as a thinking partner, always conditioned on the user's skill. Then medicine: cancer detection appears twice, unprompted, in almost identical words. Then, from one student only, emancipation — sovereignty, democratised access, freedom from the need to produce capital.

Hope is about efficiency far more than about capability, and almost never about a collective good.

Fears, by how many students raised them

Losing the capacity to think (14). Jobs (6). Water, energy and data centres (5). Unreliability — hallucination, altered inputs, context bleeding between chats (3). Information integrity (4). A market crash when the bubble deflates (2, and it was their highest-ranked item). Power concentration (3). Homogenisation and the dehumanisation of war (3). Existential risk (1, ranked last).

The widely shared fears are proximate and personal. The systemic ones are carried by three or four students each.

What goes unsaid

Assumptions that no entry argues for, and that the whole corpus rests on.

AI means a chatbot

Almost every entry treats AI as a generative text assistant. No entry mentions recommendation systems, ranking, credit scoring, insurance pricing, predictive policing or automated administrative decisions — the AI that already governs their lives. A field has been collapsed onto the product they personally use.

Cognition is zero-sum

Offloading effort is assumed to destroy capacity rather than redirect it, and it is stated as fact rather than hypothesis.

the brain is a muscle that must be trained, after all, and will weaken without it

Harm is a discipline problem

One conditional runs through the corpus: if used properly, if prompted well, if we have the will power. Responsibility sits with the individual user. This is what explains the near-total absence of governance — if the problem is willpower, law is beside the point.

Benefits are private, costs are public

Benefits are described in the first person, costs in the third. No entry connects the two: nobody notices that their own lecture summaries are a claim on the water and energy they criticise elsewhere.

The technology is weather

Adoption is something to adapt to, not something built by identifiable firms making identifiable choices — though the same students who call it irreversible also expect its market to collapse.

AI is the modern-day fire… there is nothing that can be done to undo it

Impacts are universal

People get lazy, society is destabilised, unemployment is universal. Only three entries name a who: those with access to better models, superpowers, and children. Distributional thinking — the core move of their own discipline — is largely absent.

Human intelligence has a guaranteed ceiling above the machine

An argument from origin, asserted rather than defended.

It will never replace human intelligence since it is made by it

Personal experience generalises to society

Nearly every claim about society is extrapolated from two years of using a chatbot. This is the corpus's honesty and its central weakness at the same time.

The missing entry

1of 90 entries mentions regulation — ranked last by the student who wrote it
2mention democracy
0mention a law, court, union, election or the EU AI Act

In a room of political science students, the political dimension of AI is the least-represented theme in the corpus. It follows directly from the discipline assumption above: if the harm comes from users lacking self-control, then no institution has anything to do.

How this was made

Stance and theme were coded manually from the text of each entry. The impact slider that students also filled in is not used here: 37 of 90 entries were left at the default of zero, six students never moved it, and several scores contradict their own text — “The end of the world (AGI, misalignment, Cyber/Bioweapons)” was scored +5, “Optimism regarding new opportunities” was scored −5. A share of the cohort read the slider as magnitude rather than direction. For the next round, force an interaction, relabel the scale as direction, and allow more than five minutes: the low-ranked entries are currently too thin to code.
